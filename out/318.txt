Edges were produced with llmwiki's own parser (`mcp/tools/references.py`,

is what llmwiki itself would have written.

4,427 resolved links collapse to 3,973 distinct pairs because a page may link
the same target more than once — most often its source page, cited once in the
body and once in the footer.

Most-linked pages, which is the shape you would expect of a document projection:

  wiki/sources/bh2.md          <- 844 pages
  wiki/sources/BH3FR.md        <- 623 pages
  wiki/sources/BH1org_OCR.md   <- 574 pages

1,446 of 2,222 pages have no inbound edge. Those are the element pages —
equations, notes, figures — which link outward to their document page and source
but are only linked back from prose that happens to transclude them.

================================================================================
B. The 33 dangling links are not broken pages — they are LaTeX
================================================================================

Every one is a false positive from mathematics. llmwiki's link pattern is

    _WIKI_LINK_RE = re.compile(r"(?<!!)\[(?:[^\]]*)\]\(([^)]+)\)")

and LaTeX produces `[...](...)` naturally:

    wiki/bh2/equations/bh2_EQ0083.md   ->  bh2/equations/\mu \nu
    wiki/bh2/equations/bh2_EQ0115.md   ->  bh2/equations/\kappa \lambda
    wiki/BH1org_OCR/equations/…_EQ0145 ->  BH1org_OCR/equations/\kappa

from source of the form `\left[\begin{array}{c}…\end{array}\right](\mu \nu)`.
The parser has no math awareness, so a bracket group followed by a parenthesis
group inside `$…$` reads as a link.

This is worth naming because it is systematic on a mathematics corpus rather
than random noise: 33 of 4,460 here, and it will scale with the amount of
matrix and index notation in a document.

My own `build/verify.py` reported **0** broken links over the same pages, which
looked like agreement and was not: it requires the target to end in `.md`, so
LaTeX never matches it. Two checkers, two different blind spots — llmwiki's
sees maths as links, mine cannot see a link that lacks a suffix. Neither is
wrong about the wiki; the pages are fine.

================================================================================
C. Can it run from indexing rather than as a separate step?
================================================================================

**Today it cannot, and that is why the table was empty.**

`update_references` is called from exactly one place in the whole repository:

    mcp/tools/write.py:387   await update_references(self.fs, self.kb_id, doc_id, content, dir_path)

That is the MCP write path — `create`, `edit`, `append`. The guide's claim at
`mcp/tools/guide.py:196`, "Every write automatically parses citations and
cross-references and stores them as graph edges", is true of *that* path and
only that path.

Neither `api/domain/local_processor.py` nor `api/domain/watcher.py` calls it.
So a file placed on disk by any other means — a projector, an editor, `cp`, git
— is indexed as a document and gets **no edges at all**. The links render, the
graph is empty, and nothing reports the gap.

The hosted side has `POST /v1/knowledge-bases/{kb_id}/graph/rebuild`
(`api/routes/graph.py`) with a five-minute per-KB cooldown and a lock, because
a rebuild is O(all docs × all wiki pages). There is no local equivalent exposed.

**It could run from indexing, and cheaply.** The indexer already holds exactly
what the parser needs — `content`, `relative_path`, `document_id` — at the
moment it writes the row. The measured cost here is 0.1 s for 2,222 pages;
the hosted cooldown exists because the *hosted* rebuild recomputes the whole
knowledge base, not because parsing links is expensive. Two shapes would work:

1. **Per-document, in the indexer.** After a document is indexed, parse its
   content and replace that document's outbound edges. Incremental, same cost
   as the write path already pays, and it makes externally-written files behave
   exactly like MCP-written ones.
2. **A local rebuild command.** `llmwiki reindex` already walks every file;
   having it rebuild edges at the end costs a tenth of a second at this scale.

The distinction that matters: edges are currently a property of *how a page was
written*, not of *what the page contains*. That is the defect. A page's links
are in its content, so they should be derived wherever the content is read.
