The quest for unification—that is, the desire to explain the various manifestations of inanimate nature based on a common principle of order—is a particularly dominant theme in physics. 

As early as the first half of this century, scientists succeeded in reducing the various possible forces that can act on elementary particles or macroscopic bodies to just four empirical interactions (or interaction classes). In addition to the already known interactions of gravity and electromagnetism, the weak and strong interactions were discovered at the subatomic level. 

A theory of interactions must therefore require that these four empirically known interactions be described by a unified mathematical formalism, which must be deducible from general principles, thereby establishing a higher level of analysis. For example, using the Weinberg–Salam model, it became possible to unify the electromagnetic and weak interactions group-theoretically through the symmetry $\mathrm{U}(1) \times \mathrm{SU}(2)$ into the electroweak interaction. Quantum chromodynamics, which is also under discussion and satisfies an SU(3) symmetry, would be classified as the strong interaction and is capable of explaining the relatively large number of hadrons that have been empirically discovered in the context of high-energy physics. This quantum chromodynamics, however, was conceived analogously to the well-established quantum electrodynamics.

Gravity, however, as the last of the four fundamental interactions, differs significantly in its properties from the other three. The latter are embedded in a Euclidean spacetime.
