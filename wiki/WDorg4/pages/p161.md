The path toward the geometrization of the gravitational field was first pioneered by A. EINSTEIN in his General Theory of Relativity, according to which gravity is a curvature of four-dimensional spacetime. This work was further developed by Th. KALUZA, O. KLEIN, and R. PENROSE. 

The Kaluza–Klein theory initially fell into obscurity but was reformulated with the development of the Grand Unification Theory and supersymmetry. In this modified Kaluza–Klein theory, gauge field symmetries become geometric symmetries that require additional spatial dimensions. To accommodate all known forces of interaction, an 11-dimensional space is required, consisting of 10 real spacelike dimensions and one time dimension. Seven spatial dimensions are curled up, forming a seven-dimensional sphere, and are not directly accessible. This theory is known as supergravity theory. 

However, a theory formulated in 11 dimensions has the disadvantage that it does not correctly reproduce chirality in nature, since there is an odd number of dimensions. 

This drawback is overcome by the ten-dimensional superstring theory, which has been further developed only in recent years. It turned out that quantization anomalies no longer occurred when an SO(32) or $\mathrm{E}_{8} \times \mathrm{E}_{8}$ gauge group was used. These gauge groups, in turn, include, among others, the well-known gauge groups of the strong, weak, and electromagnetic interactions. Supergravity and superstring theory have the drawback that, for the time being, they can only make predictions at energies around $10^{-33} \mathrm{~m}$ and below. Only at particle masses close to the Planck mass is a unification of all four known interactions likely to occur. It is not known how the predictions valid at high particle energies can be extended to the low-energy regime. The experimentally determinable quantum numbers of elementary particles—such as charge, spin, isospin, baryon number, and strangeness, along with their masses—do not follow from the aforementioned unification theories.
