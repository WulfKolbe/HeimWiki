"B" of $\mathrm{s}_{\mathrm{n}}$ and $\mathrm{s}_{\mathrm{n}}^{\prime}$ is not, because $B=B^{\prime}=\left\{\left(a_{1}\right),\left(a_{1}, a_{2}\right), \ldots,\left(a_{1}, \ldots, a_{n}\right)\right\}$ holds for both sets. 

In general, according to a set-theoretic mapping law, a number $\mathrm{c}_{\mathrm{n}}$ can be assigned to a set $\left\{\mathrm{a}_{1}, \ldots, \mathrm{a}_{\mathrm{n}}\right\}$, i.e., $\mathrm{c}_{\mathrm{n}}=\mathrm{f}\left\{\mathrm{a}_{1}, \ldots, \mathrm{a}_{\mathrm{n}}\right\}$.  If the elements of a set are reordered within that set, then $\mathrm{c}_{\mathrm{n}}$ should not change; that is, $\left\{\mathrm{a}_{1}, \ldots, \mathrm{a}_{\mathrm{n}}\right\}$ cannot be understood as an ordered n-tuple. The combination rule f can therefore only refer to the operations of addition (+) or multiplication (•), for which the symbol $\mathrm{o} \hat{=}+$ or $\mathrm{o} \hat{=}$ is generally used. The application of o to $\left\{\mathrm{a}_{1}, \ldots, \mathrm{a}_{\mathrm{n}}\right\}$ yields, due to the position-independence of the  elements $\mathrm{a}_{\mathrm{k}}$ with $1 \leq \mathrm{k} \leq \mathrm{n}$ for $\mathrm{o} \hat{=}+$ now yields (o) $\left\{\mathrm{a}_{1}, \ldots, \mathrm{a}_{\mathrm{n}}\right\}=\left(\sum_{\mathrm{k}=1}^{\mathrm{n}} \mathrm{a}_{\mathrm{k}}\right)$  and (o) $\left\{\mathrm{a}_{1}, \ldots, \mathrm{a}_{\mathrm{n}}\right\}=\prod_{\mathrm{k}=1}^{\mathrm{n}} \mathrm{a}_{\mathrm{k}}$ for $\mathrm{o} \stackrel{\wedge}{=}$. It should also be noted that these combinatorial operations go back to calculations with cardinal numbers, since $\left\|\mathrm{M}\left(\mathrm{M}_{\mathrm{i}}\right)\right\|=\sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{\mathrm{n}} \mathrm{m}_{\mathrm{i}}$ and $\left\|\mathrm{M}_{1} \times \mathrm{M}_{2} \times \ldots \mathrm{M}_{\mathrm{n}}\right\|=\prod_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{\mathrm{n}} \mathrm{m}_{\mathrm{i}}$ hold. The  generalized formation laws for arithmetic and geometric sequences  are thus given by the form

The question arises as to whether these derived formation laws are special cases of an even more general structure. 

Due to $\mathrm{B}=\left\{\left(\mathrm{a}_{1}\right),\left(\mathrm{a}_{1}, \mathrm{a}_{2}\right), \ldots,\left(\mathrm{a}_{1}, \ldots, \mathrm{a}_{\mathrm{n}}\right)\right\}$, the subsets of B  exhibit chain properties; for according to $\left(\mathrm{a}_{1}\right) \subset\left(\mathrm{a}_{1} \mathrm{a}_{2}\right) \subset \ldots \subset\left(\mathrm{a}_{1}, \ldots, \mathrm{a}_{\mathrm{n}}\right)$, the set on the left is always a subset of the superset on the right. In the simplest case, the respective superset is obtained by adding an element from the already existing subset on the left. 

The simplest degenerate “chain,” as defined by $\mathrm{a}_{1}=\mathrm{a}_{2}=\mathrm{a}$, consists of only the single element a, which does not allow for the formation of a chain. The following possibilities exist for forming a chain:
