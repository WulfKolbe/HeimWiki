The analysis of these primordial forms [1, Chap. IV, 1–3] shows that form a, in  intersection with four-dimensional spacetime, physically determines the gravitational phenomenon, such that the quanta of form a appear as gravitons in $\mathrm{R}_{4}$ as a result of this  intersection. In contrast, form b always appears as a phenomenon characterized, within the asymptotic cone of the spacetime structure, by quanta with geodesic null lines; that is, form b is identical to the physical concept of the photon. Through the incorporation of the real $\mathrm{R}_{3}$, the phenomenon of ponderability—that is, the existence of energetic rest masses—arises, such that forms c and d represent particle masses. While c describes electrically neutral particles, the particles of form d are electrically charged, which, according to [1, Chap. IV, 3], allows for an empirically testable statement regarding an elementary electric charge field or the fine-structure constant of light. 

The mass spectrum resulting as a solution of [1, Chap. IV, Eq. 19] [1, Eq. 27 and Eq. 27a] is unusable for describing the spectra of possible c- and d-terms because, due to its derivation, it reproduces the energy masses of all hermetic forms a through d and therefore appears as a pseudocontinuum. It was therefore necessary to seek a separation of the spectra superimposed in this solution so that the forms c and d could be examined separately. This separation of the spectra is made possible by the concept of the  lattice-core selector and polymetry, which was developed in [1, Chap. III]. If one considers the subspace structure of $\mathrm{R}_{6}$, it becomes apparent that the subspaces structuring $\mathrm{R}_{6}$ 
$\mathrm{R}_{3}\left(\mathrm{x}_{1}, \mathrm{x}_{2}, \mathrm{x}_{3}\right), \mathrm{T}_{1}\left(\mathrm{x}_{4}\right), \mathrm{S}_{2}\left(\mathrm{x}_{5}, \mathrm{x}_{6}\right)$ each define a tensorial lattice-core selector, whereby these selectors are symbolized by ${ }^{2} \overline{\mathrm{~K}}_{(\mu)}$ with indices ranging from $\mu=1$ to $\mu=3$. The overbar here indicates that a tensor is present, with the left index 2 denoting the tensor rank. 

The term “selector” here refers to the function that selects a sequence of numbers from the set of natural numbers, since—due to the indistinguishable  elementary lengths $\delta \mathrm{s}_{0}$ , in terms of coordinates, only multiples of $\delta \mathrm{s}_{0}$ can be written—for example, ${ }^{2} \overline{\mathbf{K}}_{(3)}\left(\mathrm{n}_{1}, \mathrm{n}_{2}, \mathrm{n}_{3}\right)$ can be written as the equivalent expression ${ }^{2} \overline{\mathbf{K}}_{(3)}$; n can be written as ${ }^{2} \overline{\mathbf{K}}_{(3)}$ if ${ }^{2} \bar{\kappa}_{(3)}\left(()_{1},()_{2},()_{3}\right)$ is the selector in question. The relationship with a “partial fundamental tensor” ${ }^{2} \overline{\mathrm{~g}}_{(\mu v)}={ }^{2} \bar{\gamma}_{(\mu v)}$; n  would be given by ${ }^{2} \bar{\gamma}_{(\mu v)}=\operatorname{sp}\left({ }^{2} \bar{\kappa}_{(\mu)} \times{ }^{2} \bar{\kappa}_{(v)}\right)$, which can be written in component form as $\gamma_{\mathrm{ik}}^{(\mu \nu)}=\sum_{\mathrm{m}} \kappa_{\mathrm{im}}^{(\mu)} \kappa_{\mathrm{mk}}^{(\nu)}$.
