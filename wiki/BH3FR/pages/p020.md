The scheme of additive unification is based on the various invariance groups of the strong, electromagnetic, and weak interactions. These theories and invariance groups are subsumed under a higher-symmetry group that can encompass not only the existing individual symmetries but also additional invariance properties beyond them, which, for example, can be used to describe the transformation of particles from one symmetry group into those of another. The individual interactions, which have different coupling constants, are now required to have only a single coupling constant. This is achieved by the fact that the described grand symmetry applies only  under extreme conditions at high energies of the interaction quanta, which then exhibit identical physical behavior.  At low energy levels, the grand symmetry decays into subtheories through spontaneous symmetry breaking. Higgs fields arise spontaneously in this process, and the different properties of the gauge fields become apparent. 

The Glashow–Salam–Weinberg model, which describes the electroweak interaction and its symmetry group, can be regarded as an already unified field theory of an additive nature.
