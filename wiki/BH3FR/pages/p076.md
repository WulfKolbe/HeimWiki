In fact, such group-theoretical transfer radiation can be empirically observed as neutrino states. The $\beta$ neutrino has the smallest mass here, but it is additively composed of two identical components, which may cause this neutrino to decay temporally into these components and subsequently recombine (118c). The mass spectrum (112) has a significantly larger range than the spectrum of empirically observed particle masses, which, however, are reproduced almost exactly by (112) in a numerical analysis. This seemingly excessive scope of (112) may, on the one hand, be due to the fact that the individual resonances have very different formation probabilities, which are certainly influenced to a very significant extent by the experimental technical boundary conditions. A change in these relevant boundary conditions could potentially make other particle masses from (112) empirically detectable. On the other hand, it is possible that, under the given boundary conditions of current experimental techniques, a relatively large number of terms from (112) are hidden in the noise background. A systematic search for these particles will only become possible once a spectral relation analogous to (112) is found for the full bandwidths (i.e., the temporal stability intervals) of the individual terms. For this, however, there is a heuristic approach that is not yet ready for discussion, just as there is for the anomalous magnetic spin moments.
