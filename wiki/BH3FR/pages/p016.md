Now that a particle pair could be formed, the understanding of particle transformation—which was consistent with the experiment—gained acceptance. 

In 1932, Johann von NEUMANN made another contribution to the then-current quantum mechanics. He succeeded in mathematically proving that there would never be another quantum mechanics that eliminates randomness. It had thus become impossible to assume the existence of any hidden variables that might control the processes within an atom. 

It was not until 1964 that John Bell was able to show that the system of axioms had been chosen too narrowly and that causal control of quantum events via parameters at the subatomic level could not be ruled out. 

Before the discovery of the Dirac equation, interest was focused on the  behavior of a single particle. Quantum field theory was developed to describe the dynamics of  multiple particles collectively and to account for the  creation and annihilation of particles. There was now no longer any distinction between particles and interaction fields, but rather a single unified field that, when locally quantized, produced a specific type of particle determined by the field. The various interactions between elementary particles are now caused by the associated field quanta. A continuous exchange of virtual interaction quanta takes place between the particles. Accordingly, the electromagnetic interaction is mediated by photons, the gravitational interaction by gravitons, the strong interaction by “colored”  gluons, and the weak interaction by the $\mathrm{W}^{ \pm}$ and $\mathrm{Z}^{\circ}$ bosons.  Since elementary particles of the same type are indistinguishable, they can be exchanged with one another without altering the shared field. Photons, for example, exhibit this behavior. A large number of these particles can exist at the same location, penetrating and overlapping one another. They obey Bose statistics.
