To rule out collisions between electrons on the same orbits and to prevent the electrons from crashing into the atomic nucleus due to the continuous emission of light quanta, Niels BOHR assigned the electrons distinct orbits on which they were not allowed to emit radiation. 

It was not until Erwin SCHRÖDINGER came along that an explanation for this heuristic assumption could be provided. Based on his wave equation, published in 1926, the designated electron orbits corresponded to the eigenmodes of an electron wave, which resulted as solutions to his wave equation. The sharp conflict between the particle and wave natures came to the fore once again. Max Born therefore assigned to the intensity of the wave—which resulted from the solution to the wave equation—the probability of encountering a particle in a given region of space. 

At the same time as Schrödinger, Werner HEISENBERG entered the  arena of quantum physics. As an excellent theorist, he was no longer guided by the intuitive Bohr model of the atom. Rather, his main interest lay in finding calculation rules for energy transitions within the atom. 

These rules ultimately turned out to be a mathematical calculus involving infinite matrices, in which a matrix was assigned to each particle’s position and momentum. This computational method was very difficult to apply, and the formalism described was adopted only reluctantly. A turning point came in 1926 when SCHRÖDINGER published his four “Treatises on Wave Mechanics”  and he was able to show that his wave mechanics and HEISENBERG’s matrix mechanics were two equivalent representations of the same theory. With the help of the new wave mechanics, a number of previously intractable problems in quantum theory could now be solved. The two quantum mechanical computational formalisms thus created a new dynamics that, in the realm of atomic dimensions, replaced classical physics with a physics based on quantum laws.
