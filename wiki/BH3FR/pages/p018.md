In an effort to uncover the internal structure of the atomic nucleus, John COCKCROFT suggested as early as 1928 that protons be accelerated in an electric field and then fired at atomic nuclei. According to the laws of quantum physics, the higher the energy of the accelerated particles, the better the resolving power of this microscope for examining matter. To extend the particle’s trajectory and allow more time for acceleration, Ernest O. Lawrence built the first cyclotron a few years later, in which magnetic fields forced the particles into a spiral trajectory. In 1945 and 1952, there were significant innovations in the design of particle accelerators. The Cosmotron in New York began operation in 1952, and the Betatron in California in 1954. 

A flood of new particles was discovered using this new type of machine, which were classified as hadrons (particles capable of strong interactions). In order to describe the large number of hadrons in a unified way, scientists sought organizing principles (laws of symmetry). In the early 1960s, Murray Gell-Mann developed the quark model, according to which a hadron is formed from a combination of three or two subconstituents, the quarks and antiquarks. The binding of three different quarks together results in a baryon; the combination of a quark particle with a corresponding antiparticle yields a meson. According to a proposal made by Oscar W. Greenberg in 1964, each quark type can exist in one of three forms (“colors”). The hadron itself is colorless due to the appropriately chosen colored quarks, since the individual colors cancel each other out to produce “white.” The symmetry group responsible for the quark model and, in particular, for color theory is the SU(3) group. In the mid-1960s, indirect experimental evidence was obtained for the existence of three different types of quarks (quarks with different flavors); in 1974, a fourth quark type was added to the group of three, a fifth in 1978, and a sixth in 1984.
