Approximately (12a) 46 Mpc lies, in turn, between two boundaries, namely the lower and upper reality boundaries (14), outside of which the field is not defined. The gravitational limit, as the boundary of the attractive gravitational field, explains why spiral nebulae exist but no higher-order systems do, and why there is a cosmological redshift in the spectra of extragalactic spiral nebulae with the anomalies actually observed. The lower reality boundary (14b), on the other hand, appears to be an analogue of the Schwarzschild radius, and the upper one (14a) an analogue of the Hubble radius. However, this Hubble radius does not appear here as the radius of the universe itself, but only as the radius of an optical universe within which light signals can be received by an observer at the relative center—that is, signals whose redshift does not diverge to infinity. While the lower bound decreases as the mass of the field source decreases and vanishes for a massless source, the upper bound increases. If we now consider the field of a field source consisting solely of a single elementary structure of matter (with rest mass), then this mass is simultaneously characterized by the Compton wavelength. The product of the lower reality bound and this Compton wavelength—dimensioned as an area—is indeed a function of the field source’s mass, it becomes an improper product during the transition to empty space—that is, as the mass vanishes—because the lower reality bound approaches 0, while the wavelength approaches $\infty$. However, after substituting the two factors, a series expansion can be performed and the limit as the space becomes empty can be taken, which, as a positive finite number, is a derived natural constant in area dimensions. Since this limit value $\tau$—referred to as the Metron (15)—represents the vacuum condition ($\tau$ is approximately 3/8 of the square of the Planck length), one could view this constant area difference $\tau>0$ as the sought-after geometric ultimate unit.
