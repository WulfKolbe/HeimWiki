Since the theory of supergravity is closely related to general relativity (which is described by the former in the language of quantum field theory), it is considered here to belong to the geometric branch. It is arrived at as a gauge theory of supersymmetry. It unites particles with spins as diverse as those of fermions and bosons. Through the repeated application of the supersymmetry transformation, fermions are alternately converted into bosons, and in conjunction with this, a transition occurs from one spacetime point to another. The repeated application of the global supersymmetry transformation shifts a particle in spacetime and thus leads to a Poincaré transformation.  To derive local supersymmetry from global supersymmetry, a gauge field must be introduced. The corresponding gauge particle would then be the spin-2 graviton. This introduces gravity into the theory, making the use of the term “supergravity theory” understandable. The simplest supergravity theory describes a world consisting solely of gravitons and gravitinos, whereby the gravitino (spin-3/2 particle) is formed from the graviton by a local supersymmetry transformation. 
All four fundamental interactions and as many different particles as possible are described by the most comprehensive of the extended supergravity theories, in which, additionally, global internal symmetries for particles with the same spin are transformed into local ones.
