The exchange of electrons in a total field describing them, on the other hand, changes the field’s sign. As a result, two electrons cannot exist simultaneously at the same location. They obey Fermi statistics. Wolfgang Pauli had recognized this principle early on with regard to electrons. An explanation had thus been found for the impenetrability of matter. 

The application of quantum field theory to electrodynamics (quantum electrodynamics) led to great successes after World War II. In the years that followed, however, the increasing complexity of the equations made the application of quantum field theory nearly impossible. In 1947, Richard Feynman succeeded in developing a graphical method—a kind of shorthand—that made the description of quantum-theoretical processes simpler and more straightforward again. In quantum electrodynamics, however, there was still a problem that eluded a solution for decades. In higher-order diagrams containing closed loops, divergent integrals existed. With the help of the renormalization method (truncating a physically relevant portion), a viable solution was finally found. The concept of quantum field theory is currently being applied, among other things, to the gauge theories of the strong interaction and the unified electromagnetic and weak (electroweak) interactions.
