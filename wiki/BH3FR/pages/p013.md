At the turn of the century, Max Planck was compelled to assume that the energy of thermal radiation was quantized in the form of energy packets whose magnitude was proportional to the frequency of the emitted radiation. He did this in order to derive a formula from Maxwell’s equations and the statistical motion of atoms that correctly described the thermal radiation of a body in accordance with the experimental data. 

At first, this perspective—which differed greatly from classical physics as it was understood at the time—was an anomaly that people did not quite know how to make sense of. It was Albert Einstein who was the next to extend the quantization of thermal radiation to light.  This assumption was supported by the photoelectric effect, in which electrons are ejected from a metal surface by light quanta. Until that time, light had been attributed solely wave-like properties based on Maxwell’s well-known equations. 

But if light exhibited both particle and wave nature, then perhaps electrons and atoms—as examples of massive particles—could also possess wave properties. Louis Victor Prince de Broglie was the first to formulate this idea. In 1926, Clinton Davisson and Lester Germer succeeded in providing experimental proof of this. When electrons pass through a crystal, an interference pattern forms. 

Atomic theory raised the next question. 
The question was whether electrons and atoms had a finite size or should be regarded merely as point-like entities.
