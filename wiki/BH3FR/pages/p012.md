The application of the special theory of relativity ultimately led EINSTEIN to derive the now world-famous energy-matter equivalence formula $E=m . c^{2}$. 

His next work focused on a generalization of the special theory of relativity, which he presented to the public in 1915. EINSTEIN sought the laws governing two systems accelerating relative to one another and, in doing so, encountered Riemannian geometry, which could be used to describe curved space. The connection between non-Euclidean geometry and physics was established through the formulation of the general theory of relativity (GTR): space, time, and energy were fused into a single entity. Due to the equivalence of inertial mass and gravitational mass, the gravitational field could be linked to the curvature of space. The general theory of relativity predicted the measurable deflection of a light beam in the gravitational field of large masses, the perihelion shift of Mercury’s orbit, and the slowing down of a clock’s rate in a gravitational field—all of which were subsequently confirmed. With the help of general relativity, it was also possible to make a statement about the historicity of the universe. 

Around 1920, Alexander Fridmann published a paper in which various models of the universe were presented based on Einstein’s field equations. In the case of a self-contained, finite universe, this results in a dynamic model in which the cosmos first expands and then contracts over time. Around 1930, Edwin Hubble demonstrated, using the observed spectral line shifts of distant galaxies, that our universe is in a state of expansion. Confirmation of the Big Bang hypothesis came in 1965 from two American scientists who discovered the cosmological $2,7^{\circ} K$ background radiation, a relic from the early days of the universe.
