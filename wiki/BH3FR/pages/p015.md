To account for the fact that determining the position and momentum of a particle requires the use of another particle—such as a photon—which, however, distorts the original quantities, HEISENBERG formulated the uncertainty principle. This states that the more precisely the position of a particle is determined, the more imprecise and blurred its momentum becomes. 

In 1927, a group of prominent atomic physicists met in Copenhagen. The subject of their discussions was the interpretation of  quantum mechanics. In this context, the positivist school of thought  prevailed, according to which only observable quantities should play a role in quantum physics. The position and momentum of a particle could only be specified within the limits of Heisenberg’s uncertainty principle. The choice between a particle or wave picture was determined by the measuring apparatus used (Bohr’s principle of complementarity). For decades, the Copenhagen interpretation formed a pillar of the edifice of quantum theory that was not to be shaken. 

The results of the Copenhagen discussions contradicted Marxist philosophy, according to which it is a property of matter to be an objective reality, whether within or outside our consciousness. To move away from the subjective role of the observer—the idea that a particle’s trajectory arises only through its observation and that the probability amplitude of an observed particle changes abruptly due to an observer— the concept of probability was extended from a single particle to a  multitude of particles. This statistical approach  was consistent with experimental results, such as those from the double-slit experiment. The Marxist-oriented physicists had found an interpretation for  their philosophy. 

Before 1930, there were two major theoretical frameworks that, at first glance, seemed to have no connection to one another. The SRT created by Einstein and quantum mechanics.
