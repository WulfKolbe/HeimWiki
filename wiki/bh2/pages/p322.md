Orientation is possible because, in the spherically symmetric case, $d F \sim d \eta$ holds.  Due to the double sign, $d F$ can only refer to the cross-sectional area of a semimeridian $(N S)$, so that in the case of spherical symmetry, $2 d F=R^{2} d \eta$ and $d s=R d \eta$ must be substituted with $O \leqq \eta \leqq \pi$ or $\frac{d F}{d s}=R / 2$. Finally, the pole distance $\eta$ also applies to a parallel of latitude $r=R \sin \eta$, i.e., to the horizontal intensity $\underline{B}_{H}= \pm A \sigma R^{2} w \sin \eta$. Here, $\sigma R^{2} w=D$ is the angular momentum density of the rotating mass, so that the shorthand form $\underline{B}_{H}= \pm A D \sin \eta$ can be used. Since, due to the opposite signs, $\underline{B}_{H}$ is in opposite directions on the two semimeridians of a great circle, the ratio $\underline{B}_{V}:\left(2 \underline{B}_{H}\right)$ must be identical to $\operatorname{ctg} \eta$, which yields the vertical intensity $\underline{B}_{V}=2 \underline{B}_{H}$ ctg $\eta= \pm 2 A D \cos \eta$ , whereas the total intensity must be understood as the vector sum $\underline{\vec{B}}=\overrightarrow{\underline{B}}_{V}+2 \underline{\vec{B}}_{H}$. Because of $\vec{B}_{V} \perp \vec{B}_{H} \quad$, $\quad \underline{B}^{2}=\underline{B}_{V}^{2}+4 \underline{B}_{H}^{2}=4 A^{2} D^{2}\left(\cos ^{2} \eta+\sin ^{2} \eta\right)$ holds, so $\pm \underline{B}=2 A D$. Finally, according to $\eta+\varphi=\pi / 2$, the polar displacement can be expressed in terms of latitude $\varphi$ (relative to the  equator $\varphi=0$), which yields $\sin \eta=\cos \varphi$ and $\cos \eta=\sin \varphi$, that is, to the  values $2 \underline{B}_{H}=\underline{B} \cos \varphi$, $\underline{B}_{V}=\underline{B} \sin \varphi$, and $\underline{B}=2 A D$. 

The derivation of $\underline{B}$ assumed that the isotropic spherical material has no magnetic properties. In the general  case, however, on the one hand there is a magnetic permeability $\mu$ (which is usually a spatial function), while, on the other hand, a portion of the rotating mass may consist of an electrically neutral gas plasma of the same density, which is usually the case for astronomical objects. If such a plasma rotates in the primary permanent  magnetic field (106), this must result in a hydromagnetic generator effect, i.e., secondary electric currents are induced in the plasma, whose magnetic fields in turn counteract the inducing source, so that the primary permanent field can be dissipated in this way.
