If these conditions are met, resonance may occur, specifically according to structural principle (107). If, on the other hand, $E_{R}$ occurs in the form of a deeply inelastic collision in which the configuration zones $j=4$ through $j=2$ are penetrated and $E_{R} \gg\left(M_{L}-M_{x}\right) c^{2}$ holds, then (107) is bypassed if, at the same time, $T<\theta_{x}$ holds for the transfer time. In such a case, $M_{x}$ is literally “heated up” chaotically by a very large number of additional $( \pm p)$ in a form that is insufficient for (107). Under the given conditions, this amount of energy cannot be structured and is therefore, in accordance with the principle of conservation of momentum, emitted in the form of several (at least two) high-energy rays of corpuscular nature, whereby these corpuscular rays appear to be arranged with high symmetry in the $R_{3}$ relative to the correlation center due to the principle of momentum conservation. The specific  particle pairs $(\varepsilon=+1$ and $\varepsilon=-1)$ and the proportion  of $\gamma$ quanta ( $b$ hermiticity) depends both on the structure of the term $(v)_{x}$ excited in this way as well as on $E_{R}$ and the boundary conditions of the deeply inelastic collision  $T<\theta_{x}$. After the emission, $(v)_{x}$ is restored from the process. In fact, within the empirical framework of high-energy physics, such corpuscular jets have been observed.

This, however, renders the auxiliary concepts of leptoneutrinos and baryoneutrinos  superfluous.
