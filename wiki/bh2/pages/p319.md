Since in $a \operatorname{rot}(\vec{C}+\vec{g})=\vec{x} \times \vec{y}$ the vector fields, as physical quantities, must have the same dimensions, only the proportionality factors $\sqrt{\mu_{0}}$ and $\sqrt{\beta}$ are considered in $\vec{C} \sim \vec{H}$ and $\vec{g} \sim \vec{\mu}$; only the proportionality constants $\sqrt{\mu_{0}}$ and $\sqrt{\beta}$ are considered; if $\beta$ is the unknown  natural constant in the propagation law of gravitational field perturbations  $\operatorname{divgrad} \vec{p}+\alpha \beta \ddot{\vec{p}}=\overrightarrow{0}$ (not to be confused with $\beta$ from (105a)).  Thus, we have $\vec{C}=\vec{H} \sqrt{\mu_{0}}$ and $\vec{g}=\vec{\mu} \sqrt{\beta}$ must be set, which, on the one hand, is related to $\vec{G}=\overrightarrow{0}$ and $\vec{E}=\overrightarrow{0}$ yields the relations $\operatorname{rot} \vec{C}=\varrho \vec{v} \sqrt{\mu_{0}}$ and $\operatorname{rot} \vec{g} \sim$  $\sim \sigma \vec{v} \sqrt{\beta}$, that is, $a \operatorname{rot}(\vec{C}+\vec{g})=\vec{v}\left(a \varrho \sqrt{\mu_{0}}+\sigma \sqrt{\beta}\right)$, and on the other hand, when compared with $a \operatorname{rot}(\vec{C}+\vec{g})=\vec{x} \times \vec{y}$, yields $\vec{x} \times \vec{y}=\vec{v}\left(\varrho a \sqrt{\mu_{0}}+\sigma \sqrt{\beta}\right)$. Here, $\vec{v}$ appears as the vector product of two polar vectors, which, however, characterizes a rotational motion. If $\vec{r}$ is the  radius vector of this motion and $\vec{\omega}$' is its angular frequency, then  $\vec{v}=\vec{r} \times \vec{\omega}$' holds. Since a charge field rotating at $\vec{\omega}$' $\varrho$—as a circular current—induces a magnetic field in any case, we assume that there are no free, uncompensated charges at all in the mass distribution $\sigma$, so that $\varrho=0$ may be assumed. However, this yields  from $\operatorname{arot}(\vec{C}+\vec{g})=\vec{x} \times \vec{y} \quad$ the simple phenomenological relationship $a \operatorname{rot}\left(\vec{H} \sqrt{\mu_{0}}+\vec{\mu} \sqrt{\beta}\right)=\left(\vec{r} \times \overrightarrow{\omega^{\prime}}\right) \sigma \sqrt{\beta}$, in which only $\beta \neq 0$ is known from $\beta$. If one assumes $\beta=-\underline{\beta}<0$, then the transverse wave equation $\operatorname{divgrad} \vec{p}=\alpha \underline{\beta} \ddot{\vec{p}}$ with the propagation speed $\omega^{2} \alpha \underline{\beta}=1$ would indeed result for the propagation law of gravitational field perturbations, but because of $\sqrt{\beta}=i \sqrt{\underline{\beta}}$, the rotor coordinate transformation would be complex, with the real part $\operatorname{rot} \vec{H}=\overrightarrow{0}$ and the imaginary part  $\operatorname{arot} \vec{\mu}=\sigma \vec{r} \times \vec{\omega}$, which corresponds to the gravitational and electrodynamic laws for $\vec{G}=\overrightarrow{0}$, $\vec{E}=\overrightarrow{0}$, and $\varrho=0$. If, on the other hand, one assumes $\beta>0$, then divgrad $\vec{p}+\alpha \beta \ddot{\vec{p}}=\overrightarrow{0}$ does not describe a wave equation, but rather a $R_{4}$potential equation with the propagation speed $\omega$ of the potential; however, the rotor relation remains real, so that in this phenomenological equation, in the case of $\beta>0$ the fields $\vec{H}$ and $\vec{\mu}$ are mutually dependent.
