It is entirely conceivable that, for some subphysical structural reasons, matter cosmogony began several $10^{10}$ years ago (possibly $T$ - $T_{k}$ between $2 \cdot 10^{10}$ and $6 \cdot 10^{10}$ years) reached catastrophic intensity, such that the entire energy-mass of a spiral nebula or a galaxy cluster of extremely high density collapsed into a  relatively small $R_{3}$-volume, only to then expand explosively. These cosmogonic objects would thus have to possess the luminosity of a galaxy but exhibit, optically, the quasi-stellar properties of a point-like light source. Since, due to $D_{M} \approx D_{k}$, $c=c_{k}$ is satisfied to a very good approximation, there would in any case be an absolute visibility limit for astronomical objects 
$R=c\left(T-T_{k} \pm \Delta T_{k}\right)$; however, due to $\sigma>0$ in (46), the radius of the optical space segment is always $y<R$. In fact, quasi-stellar objects are indeed observed as quasars in the range $s<y$  , whose $z$ values, according to (45), indicate distances of several $10^{9}$ light-years.
