With the publication of the first volume of *Heim: Elementary Structures of Matter (1979)*, the foundations for a completely new approach to elementary particle physics were already laid. With the publication of this second volume, Heim’s theory of the elementary structures of matter is now available in a very comprehensive form, though it should not be regarded as a final conclusion. The significance and distinctive nature of the present exposition of Heim’s theory are already evident from brief comparisons with other theories of elementary particle physics. 

Quantum field theory, when combined with the application of symmetry principles (including that of gauge fields), can be regarded as the concept that unifies the currently best-known theories of elementary particle physics. These include quantum electrodynamics, which describes the interaction between light and matter; the Weinberg–Salam model, which unifies the electromagnetic and weak interactions; the quark model, which pertains to the strong interactions; and finally, the extended supergravity theory, which is expected to unify all four interaction fields and provide a description of all elementary particles discovered experimentally to date, while simultaneously applying the principle of broken symmetries. 

The quark model can presumably describe the internal structure of hadrons. Details regarding the internal structure of quarks or leptons themselves—to the extent that they exist—cannot currently be specified.
