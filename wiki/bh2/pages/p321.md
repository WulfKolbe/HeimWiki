Furthermore, let $\varrho=0$ be  and let $\underline{\vec{B}}$ be examined only at the surface of this sphere.  Under these conditions, it is further assumed that the  spherical volume rotates about an axis of rotation $N S$, such that $r$ is the distance of a surface point from the axis of rotation—that is, the radius of a circle of latitude—for which the angle $\eta$ is used as the polar distance. Furthermore, let $\vec{F}$ be the circular cross-sectional area of a polar meridian, and $\vec{s}$ a line bounding this area that runs parallel to the polar meridian. In any case, $\vec{r} \perp \vec{w}$ and $\vec{F} \|(\vec{r} \times \vec{w})$ hold, so that, when differentiated, $\operatorname{rot} \underline{\vec{B}} d \vec{F}=2 A \sigma(\vec{r} \times \vec{w}) d \vec{F}_{*}= \pm 2 A \sigma w r d F$ results, or, when integrated according to an integral theorem, $\pm 2 A \iint \sigma r w d F=\iint \operatorname{rot} \underline{\vec{B}} d \vec{F}=\oint \underline{\vec{B}} d \vec{s}$ results.
