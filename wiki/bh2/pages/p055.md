Let there be a mass system $C$ (spiral nebula system) with mass $M_{0}$ and a  gravitational boundary $\varrho$, in which an observation point $S$  is located at a distance $s_{0} \gg 0$ from the gravitational center of $C$ ( $S$ is, however, still within $\varrho$), so that $s_{0}<\varrho$ holds. 

If a mass $\mu$ of $S$ be moved to a location $S(\varrho)$ on $r=\varrho$, then $\mu$ must acquire kinetic energy corresponding to the gravitational potential of the field $g<0$ between $\varrho$ and $s_{0}$. However, if $\mu$ is brought into the region $r>\varrho$, then according to relation (11c), the field $g>0$ (due to $r>\varrho$) must accelerate the mass $\mu$ away from $C$. In this case, however, $\mu$ would have to receive a constant increase in kinetic energy, which would have to be supplied in some way by $C$. If, on the other hand, one assumes that $g>0$ exists only latently beyond $\varrho$—that is, that there is no radial acceleration $g>0$  is directed away from $C$ and $g>0$ only becomes effective in the sense of a deceleration  when $\mu$ is to be brought closer to the system $C$ from $r>\varrho$,  then this difficulty is eliminated. Beyond $\varrho$, under this assumption, no repulsive acceleration acts on $\mu$, but energy must be expended if $\mu$ is to be brought from $r>\varrho$ closer to the reference frame. With this assumption, it is apparently possible, in the case of a static or quasi-static universe, to explain the observed redshift of extragalactic spiral nebula spectra using Equation (11). In addition to the mass system $C$, there exists, at a sufficiently large distance, another system $C^{\prime}$ with mass $M_{0}^{\prime}$ and $\varrho$ '.
