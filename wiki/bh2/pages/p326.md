Assuming $\beta>0$ and thus (106), there would also have to be cosmic magnetic fields that arise from the gravitational rotation of cosmic systems. In particular, a galactic magnetic field of type (106a) must exist, because all stellar, dust, and gaseous galactic matter with $v^{2} \sim 1 / r$ at intervals of $0<r \leqq R_{G}$ around a galactic center, where $R_{G}$ is the galactic radius. Even at $v_{\text {min }}^{2} \sim 1 / R_{G}$, the circular velocity exceeds the speed of sound of the cosmic intergalactic gas, so that the gaseous galactic matter, in interaction with the intergalactic gas, forms circularly orbiting galactic compression fronts with $v^{2} \sim 1 / r$. Now, if this gaseous matter is ionized by the influence of stellar radiation and, in addition, a galactic magnetic field of type (106a) is acting, then the inductive influence of the field on the electrically charged matter moving $v$  perpendicular to the field, this matter would, due to  $v \sim r_{m} \neq r$, be moved at $v$ to a different distance $r_{m}$ from the galactic center. However, this would mean that at  some distance $r$, the gaseous, electrically charged matter of the  circular compression fronts would move at a velocity $v^{\prime} \neq v$  , so that a speed difference $V=\left|v^{\prime}-v\right|$ arises, whereas this is not possible for compact stellar matter. Thus, due to $V \neq 0$, individual stars must temporarily pass through such compression fronts. In this process, the interplanetary gas density between the central star and a planet could increase by several orders of magnitude, which in turn implies a certain scattering of starlight, so that the solar constant of the planet in question may decrease by an amount corresponding to this scattering.
