Earth $D_{e}=\sigma_{e} R_{e}^{2} w_{e} \approx 5,1982 \cdot 10^{12}\left[k g m^{-1} s^{-1}\right]$. With $A$, the geomagnetic permanent field is then given by $B_{e} \approx \mu S(t) \cdot 1,552[G]$.  Since, with the exception of a small amount of material—which, relative to the Earth’s total mass, is generally insignificant—the outer fracture zone layer lies below the Curie point; but the vast majority of the Earth’s mass lies more or less well above this Curie point, $\mu \approx 1$ can be assumed globally. If $S=1$ were true, then $B_{e}=1,552[G]$ would have to be true,  which, however, in no way corresponds to the average geomagnetic measurement, for which $B_{M} \approx 0,626[G]<B_{e}$ is reported. For this  reason, based on $S<1$, it must be concluded that geomagnetic  secular variations exist; that is, it seems justified to conclude that  at least the outer core of the Earth is in a gaseous plasma state. According to this line of reasoning, the current $S$ factor would be $S_{g}=0,403$ , which is entirely consistent with measurements of paleomagnetic rock remanence, which empirically indicate values of $B_{\text {max }}$ between 1.2 and 1.6 gauss. This, in turn, is consistent with the theoretical value $B_{e} \approx 1,552[G]$ at $\mu=S=1$. However, (106a) cannot account for the reversal of the geomagnetic field and the time-varying declination between the field and the Earth’s angular momentum axis—a phenomenon that can also be inferred from the orientations of paleomagnetic rock remanence. It is conceivable, however, that the secondary inductions $S$  of the permanent primary field could excite a tertiary-type generator effect in the boundary layer between the Earth’s mantle and outer core, whose field also varies over time in direction when the geometric shape of this boundary layer changes over time. At the same time, the geodynamics of plate tectonics in the Earth’s outer crust would likely need to be taken into account here. Furthermore, local variations in the field must occur here because the temperature of this material is below the Curie point, which must result in specific variations in $\mu$. Thus, $\mu \gg 1$ can form over $F e_{3} O_{4}$ deposits. Short-term variations caused by external influences of an inductive nature (the solar magnetic field or charged particle streams from the solar wind) can also be detected, but these have nothing to do with (106).
