If we substitute the data $\hat{S}\left(e^{-}\right)$into (112), it becomes apparent that (112) reproduces the empirical value $m_{e}$ just as accurately as (29) reproduces the field $e_{ \pm}$. Of the elementary physical constants $\hbar, \gamma, \varepsilon_{0}$  and $\mu_{0}$ used, only $\hbar$ known to seven decimal places, but this also applies to $c, m_{e}$ and $e_{-}$. Therefore, in (29) and (112), one can substitute the  empirical values $e_{-}$ and $m_{e}$ in (29) and (112), and—by calculating “backwards,” so to speak—numerically determine the natural constants $\gamma, \varepsilon_{0}$ and $\mu_{0}$ numerically  with the same precision, using  $c \sqrt{\varepsilon_{0} \mu_{0}}=1$ and $R_{-} \sqrt{\varepsilon_{0}}=\sqrt{\mu_{0}}$ as well (see Appendix). 

The function $f(N)$ in (108) increases monotonically according to (110) by $N$,  unlike $M(N)$. Thus, $M$ initially increases from $N=0$ to $N_{m}$ in a strictly monotonically increasing manner, such that within this interval, $M(N+1)-$ is always less than $-M(N)>0$. However, once $N_{m}$ is exceeded, this increase is countered by $M\left(N_{m}+1\right)-M\left(N_{m}\right)<0$ to rise again from $N_{m}+1$ and reach $M\left(N_{s}\right) \approx M\left(N_{m}\right)$ again at $N_{s}>N_{m}$ ,  whereupon a further $M$ rise follows for $N>N_{s}$ until such an interruption occurs. In contrast to $f(N)$, relation (112) thus describes a stepped curve in which the structural principle (107) repeats with each step break.
