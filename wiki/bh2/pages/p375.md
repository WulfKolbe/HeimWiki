If it were possible to break down an elementary particle into these subconstituents, then the protosimplex configurations of the relevant subconstituents would inevitably form new configuration zones of a fourfold contouring of space (the most probable state), which in turn proves to be a component within the totality of logically possible forms of complex hermeticism described by (112) or as a $b$ field. The $G$ subconstituents are therefore defined only as internal structural components through their  mutual interdependencies, but not individually. This likely renders the requirement of “confinement” and the concept of the gluon—which is conditioned by this requirement—superfluous. 

Although scattering experiments involving $\beta$neutrinos on protons seem to support the quark model, since such scatters point to $G=3$ internal structural elements, this conclusion is by no means compelling; for, due to the necessarily different densities in  the configuration zones $j \leqq 3$ of the particle volume $(j=4$  (which generally does not seem relevant to such scatterings here), such an internal structure consisting of three structurally distinct regions becomes plausible in any case, especially since, in the case of $p$ , due to $k=2$, $G=3$ baryonic subconstituents must exist in any case. The scattering experiments mentioned can therefore be regarded as at least equally strong support for the structural theory. If, on the other hand, one attempts to determine the mass contributions of the individual subconstituents, it becomes apparent that, according to the quark model, quarks with different mass properties must exist in every proton, which manifests itself in very large mass distributions. This fact is likely to remain incomprehensible within the framework of the quark model. Within the framework of the structural theory of the internal correlation dynamics of condenser fluxes, which are correlated into an integral flux system, such a finding becomes easily understandable. Since the internal correlation structure encompasses all subconstituents, the internal mass distribution at any given time depends on the dynamic state of the respective flux phase, so that these masses of the individual subconstituents must appear to vary over time in the framework of dynamic structural theory.
