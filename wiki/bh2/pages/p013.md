Regarding the notation used in the formulae, it should be noted that, in general, the standard defined by DIN was followed, insofar as the scope of application pertained to the calculus of infinitesimals $\tau=0$. Deviations from this standard occurred in only three instances for reasons of practicality. Thus, $C$ is represented as a matrix using the symbol `{ }^{\text {^ }}` in the form $\hat{C}$. If the matrix elements $C_{i k}$ belong to the complex algebraic field, then this means  $C_{i k} \neq C_{i k}^{*}$; however, if a transposition is adjoint to this complex conjugation, this is expressed by the symbol × in the form $\left(C_{i k}\right)^{\times}=C_{k i}^{*}$. Thus, if $\hat{C}=\hat{C}^{*}$ is a matrix over the field of real numbers, then $\hat{C}^{\times}$ simply denotes the transposed form, but in the complex case, $\hat{C}^{\times}$ becomes the complex-conjugate transposition. 

Instead of the commutator symbol $[a, b]=a b-b a$, which is defined by the norm, the old symbol $(a \times b)_{ \pm}=a b \pm b a$ is used  (which, with the superscripts, also applies to the anticommutator); for $[a, b]$ had already been assigned by the author to a different concept in earlier studies. Finally, instead of the very  practical nabla operator, the old operator symbols div,  grad, and rot in $R_{3}$ are also used, because when working in higher-dimensional spaces $R_{n}$ the operator is indexed by the number of dimensions $n$ in accordance with $\operatorname{div}_{n}, \operatorname{grad}_{n}$ and $\operatorname{rot}_{n}$. Here, however, $\operatorname{div}_{n}$ describes only the scalar divergence of a vector field in $R_{n}$. If it is the vector divergence of a tensor field with tensor rank $m$ by 1, this is denoted by $\overline{\operatorname{div}}_{n}$, whereas  the tensor divergence of the relevant field—which extends the tensor degree $m$ to $m+1$ is expressed by $\widehat{\operatorname{div}}_{n}$. However, these operators are rarely needed in this text. 

In the domains of validity $\tau>0$, on the other hand, a notation not covered by DIN is used, although it was explained in Volume I.
