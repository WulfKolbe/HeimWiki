(d) In the macroscopic realm, the fields of electromagnetism, gravitation, and short-range forces exist. 
$\left(\mathrm{d}_{1}\right)$ The electromagnetic field is described by Maxwell’s law of electromagnetic induction in a gauge-invariant manner. 
$\left(\mathrm{d}_{2}\right)$ Empirically, all that is known about the gravitational field is that its scalar field function (the square of a circular velocity) is directly proportional to the mass generating the field and inversely proportional to the distance from the center of gravity, and that the field is not gauge-invariant (NEWTON). 
$\left(\mathrm{d}_{3}\right)$ In the nuclear realm, there are short-range fields. 
These last three empirical statements $\left(\mathrm{d}_{1}\right),\left(\mathrm{d}_{2}\right)$ and $\left(\mathrm{d}_{3}\right)$ reflect the nature of such fields only within the limits of measurability and are probably not exact. 

In addition to this empirical basis for our theoretical investigations, let us briefly mention some empirical evidence regarding the elementary structures of matter under discussion. Experimental investigations in high-energy physics reveal a spectrum of elementary particles, to which there corresponds a mirror-symmetric spectrum of antiparticles. It seems highly probable to us that these particles are in fact the ultimate units of matter;  for despite increasing technological sophistication in experimental high-energy physics, collision studies have shown that the colliding particles do not decay into any  subsystems, but rather form pairs (particle-antiparticle). For this reason, we consider it reasonable to conclude that these elementary corpuscles should not only be regarded as fundamental units of matter,  but also to interpret them as permitted transitions from free energy to ponderable matter, a view that is further supported by their already relatively extensive spectrum.
