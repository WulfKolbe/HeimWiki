The worldline distance between two infinitesimally adjacent world points, i.e., $d \vec{s}=\sum_{k=1}^{6} \vec{e}_{k} d x_{k}=\sum_{k=1}^{6} \dot{\vec{x}}_{k} d t$, can be related to the time element $d t$ and thus defines a world velocity  $d \vec{s}=\vec{Y} d t$, which can be explicitly expressed in the form $\vec{Y}=\vec{v}+i \vec{u}$ if $\vec{v}$ is the relative velocity in $R_{3}$ and  $u^{2}=c^{2}+\dot{\varepsilon}^{2}+\dot{\eta}^{2}$. Thus, for $\dot{\varepsilon}=\dot{\eta}=0$, $u=c$ holds. The infinitesimally small distance $d \vec{s}$ is a worldline element, so that for the direction cosine, the angle $\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{k}$ between the worldline tangents and the $x_{k}$ generally holds $\cos \alpha_{k} d s=d x_{k}$ or $Y \cos \alpha_{k}=\dot{x}_{k}$. From this, it immediately becomes clear that for all indexings $j \neq 4$, $\cos \alpha_{j}=0$ or  $2 \alpha_{j}=\pi$, even though in general $\cos \alpha_{j} \neq 0$ holds.
