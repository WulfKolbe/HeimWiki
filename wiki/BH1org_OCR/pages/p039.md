On the other hand, if the phenomenological tensor $T_{i k}$ is the unified  energy density tensor of an electromagnetic field, and if one neglects the gravitational component with $\vec{p}=\overrightarrow{0}$, so that $T_{i k}=V_{i k}=V_{k i}^{*}$  becomes the Hermitian canonical energy-density tensor of the field $\left(d_{1}\right)$, then $V_{i k}$ must be divergence-free by virtue of (a). In general  relativity, the $g_{i k}^{(1)}$ of Riemannian geometry  are interpreted as tensorial gravitational potentials  due to the geodesic equation. Since only one structure tensor can unambiguously be constructed in this Riemannian geometry that is divergence-free and depends solely on the $g_{i k}^{(1)}$ as well as their first and second partial derivatives with respect to the spacetime coordinates, namely $R_{i k}^{(1)}-\frac{1}{2} g_{i k}^{(1)} R^{(1)}$, the following is set in General Relativity: $R_{i k}^{(1)}-\frac{1}{2} g_{i k}^{(1)} R^{(1)} \sim V_{i k}$. General Theory of Relativity is now interpreted to mean that the phenomenological tensor—which is proportional to the divergence-free structure tensor—acts as a gravitational field source, exciting the structure field of Riemannian geometry, which in turn is interpreted as the gravitational field. 

This fundamental equation of the General Theory of Relativity has certainly proven its worth in the macroscopic range and provides a relatively good description of a number of macroscopic physical phenomena of a cosmological nature.
