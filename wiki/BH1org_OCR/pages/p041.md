However, the variety of solutions appears too limited for this to be a law of nature. Furthermore, (as was already the case in general relativity) the entire realm of experience (c) was excluded from consideration. A theoretical reference to (c) from a unified structural theory alone cannot apparently be expected, because (c) pertains to a realm of experience that is logically entirely distinct from that which characterizes the macroscopic field continua. 

According to (1b), the $\Gamma_{k m}^{i}$ must correspond to the components of interaction forces in the $R_{3}{ }^{\prime}$ ; however, these appear as components of a mixed-variant tensor field of the third degree only with respect to regular affinities, and of these, only the Hermitian components are relevant due to the summation in (1b). 

Although the theoretical approach from (1) to (1b) satisfies the empirical principle (b)—since (1b) can be directly deduced from a variational theorem that expresses the extremal principle (b)— however, this approach does not necessarily satisfy (a) and does not satisfy (c) at all. Furthermore, due to $g_{i k} \neq g_{\mathrm{ki}}^{*}$, the state of a momentary $R_{3}$ structure appears to depend on the entire history leading up to it. Provided that these shortcomings of the approach are of such a small order of magnitude that they do not appear empirically, it would be necessary to investigate  when a correction is warranted. However, this will prove unnecessary,  because it can be shown below that these defects cancel each other out when the empirical principle (c) is taken into account. The  field equations $\left(\mathbf{d}_{\mathbf{1}}\right)$ and $\left(\mathbf{d}_{\mathbf{2}}\right)$ of the empirical theorem (d) are, in any case, contained in (1), because the $g_{i k}$ components are part of a general  tensor interaction potential, which must in any case  include (d).
