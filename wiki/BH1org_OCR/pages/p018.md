A description of the material world can be optimal only if the descriptive methodology is adapted to the quantitative nature of matter—that is, if it is itself quantitative in nature. For this reason, only a mathematical schema can be considered the optimal descriptive methodology. Although mathematics is a self-contained system bound by only a few axioms, this system in and of itself is by no means a schema that can be regarded as a representation of this material world. A mathematical description must always aim to discover a mathematical schema such that this schema represents an analogue to the structures of the quantitative material world—which, however, cannot be possible without certain preconditions. 

The necessary prerequisite for discovering this mathematical schema must stem from the nature of the material world itself; that is, an inductive basis must be found in physical empiricism, consisting of empirically well-founded and quantitatively formulated physical statements. This should consist of as small a number as possible of empirically well-founded physical principles of the greatest possible universality. 

As such an inductive empirical basis for a mathematical description of the material world, we used the following propositions: 
(a) There are general conservation principles, such as those of energy, momentum, or electric charge. 
(b) There are extremal principles, such as the law of entropy in the macroscopic realm, which can be formulated using variational theorems. 
(c) Physical actions are always integer multiples of the smallest action $h$ , so that the atomic structure of matter and the nonexistence of an energy continuum must be regarded as consequences of this quantum principle.
