Furthermore,  each particle possesses a measurable inertial rest mass and an  average lifetime, after which, with the exception of the particles known as electrons and protons, these particles transmute into other terms of the spectrum through radioactive decay. Conversely, interactions between these elementary particles can also be observed, which in turn give rise to elementary particles of the empirical spectrum.  In these decay and reaction processes, a whole series of conservation laws for certain particle properties can be observed, some of which, as conservation principles (a), always hold, while other conservation laws are violated under certain conditions. Consequently, the symmetries corresponding to such conservation laws also appear to be broken. 

In addition to these empirical elementary particles, there are also neutrino states that can accompany decay and reaction processes, but which can currently be detected only indirectly and are not yet quantitatively measurable. 

In the well-known manner, the vector-analytic differential equations from $\left(\mathrm{d}_{1}\right)$ can be substituted into one another, which, using vector-analytic operator theorems, yields the propagation law  of electromagnetic induction as a transverse wave equation  in a charge-free vacuum with the propagation speed  $c \sqrt{\varepsilon_{0} \cdot \mu_{0}}=1$ (the speed of light). In the undisturbed case, the wave zones are spherically symmetric in $R_{3}$ due to $c=$ const,  while the frequency $v$ and the wavelength $\lambda$ are related by $\lambda v=c$. With respect to inertial frames moving at a constant speed $v$ relative to one another, $\lambda$ depends on the respective reference frame, whereas the spherical symmetry of the wave zones remains invariant when the inertial frames change. This establishes the electromagnetic principle of relativity, which also requires the invariance of the wave equation. This, in turn, provides as a transformation—
