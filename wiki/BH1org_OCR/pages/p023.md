Even the currently empirically known set of $M q$ is relatively extensive and presents an extremely confusing picture of a wide variety of properties. At first glance, a  uniform description seems impossible, especially since there is no simple system that could serve as a starting point for such a description. Regardless of this phenomenological picture, however, all these $M q$ must be states of energy of whatever nature, such that every $M q$ must be assigned a pon-derable or imponderable inertial mass according to the energy-matter equivalence derived from $\left(\mathrm{d}_{1}\right)$. 

A deductive, unified description of the $M q$ must be based on a property that is common to all $M q$ in equal measure and defines these $M q$ by the fact that this property does not disappear in any $M q$. Here, once again, inertial mass or the  duration of existence presents itself as a suitable candidate. With regard to the duration of existence, we do not consider this to be a suitable starting point. Although this time is always nonzero if a structure is to appear to exist at all, this time interval presupposes, as a deductive starting point, concrete knowledge of the spacetime structure of the $M q$  ; moreover, for some $M q$, this time is at least as long as the age of matter itself. Therefore, the concept of the inertial mass of all $M q$ seems to us to be a suitable deductive starting point. 

From $\left(\mathrm{d}_{2}\right)$ and the concept of inertial forces, one can infer—in the well-known  manner described by A. EINSTEIN, a further equivalence principle of inertia and gravitation can be deduced in the well-known manner, such that every inertial effect is equivalent to a gravitational effect, and consequently every inertial mass must be regarded as the source of a gravitational field.
