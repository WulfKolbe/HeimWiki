If one is to attempt to describe, in a unified form, as comprehensive a range of the material world as possible, then the basis for such a description can only be a unified quantitative theory of the properties of material ultimate units. 

The empirical data obtained at particle accelerators in high-energy physics now indicate that the possible transitions from  free energy to ponderable matter are such material fundamental units—that is, elementary structures of matter. At least given the current state of high-energy physics technologies, it was apparently not possible to empirically isolate any subconstituents from which these elementary particles might be composed, although neutrino scatters point to internal structures. 

An apparent contradiction to the view of elementary particles as fundamental units of matter is evidently the empirical fact that nearly all of these particles are subject to radioactive decay processes. If one assumes the existence of any subconstituents, no analogy to atomic or nuclear structure is discernible; for the binding energy in the case of atomic or nuclear structures is always significantly smaller than the total mass of the relevant atomistic complex system, whereas in the case of elementary particles (based on an analysis of the decay processes), this binding energy would correspond approximately to the particle mass. In contrast to the dynamics of atomic or nuclear structures, the dynamics of elementary particles must therefore exhibit relativistic characteristics. 

The model currently accepted by most experts for describing elementary particles is the so-called “quark model.” Here, for reasons of group theory, “quarks” are postulated as the fundamental units of matter; there were originally supposed to be three of them, and they constitute all elementary particles
